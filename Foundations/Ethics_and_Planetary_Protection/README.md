# Ethics and Planetary Protection

*Foundational ethical framework — November 2026*

## Our starting point: stewardship across time

Life on Earth is the product of billions of years of evolution. We inherit a living world shaped by an extraordinary history, and our decisions today may influence its future for millions or billions of years to come.

Rocks2Space recognizes a responsibility to life across time: **to honor its deep past, protect its living present, and preserve possibilities for its distant future.**

These responsibilities should be pursued together whenever possible. Ideally, decisions should benefit life today while strengthening its prospects for tomorrow.

**When responsibilities conflict, we advocate prioritizing the protection of life in the present and near term, while remaining conscious of consequences for the deep future.**

This is not a rejection of long-term thinking. It is the foundation of responsible long-term thinking. The future of life depends on the survival and flourishing of the biosphere that exists today. Our commitment to the distant future must strengthen, rather than diminish, our stewardship of Earth.

**Protect life now. Preserve its possibilities for the future.**

## The responsibility of capability

**With the power to influence life's future comes the responsibility to consider it.** Rocks2Space explicitly adopts this philosophical position. It is a normative commitment of the initiative, not a conclusion established by science or an obligation imposed on every collaborator.

If humanity can investigate ways for life to survive beyond Earth, we believe it should examine those possibilities seriously. Responsibility also includes knowing when **not** to act: scientific feasibility alone would not justify biological introduction into another environment. Earth's biosphere comes first, and any contemplated intervention must confront possible indigenous life, irreversible ecological effects, applicable legal obligations, and legitimate international governance.

The flowering and *going to seed* metaphor expresses a question about life's future, not a presumption that life arrived here through panspermia or that dispersal is always desirable.

## The ethical tension: past, present, and future

- **Deep past — inheritance:** Earth's living diversity carries an evolutionary history that cannot simply be recreated once lost.
- **Present — living responsibility:** organisms, ecosystems, and communities face harms and needs now; these are not expendable for hypothetical distant gains.
- **Deep future — continuity:** present choices can foreclose or preserve possibilities for future life, sometimes irreversibly.

The past supplies context and value; the present calls for protection; the future calls for foresight. We should try to think across all three timescales simultaneously, without treating distant speculation as permission to neglect immediate duties.

**Near-term priority is a presumption, not a ban on long-term prevention.** Sometimes preventing a foreseeable, irreversible future harm requires action today. Assess the strength of evidence, magnitude and timing of harm, reversibility, affected parties, and whether safer alternatives exist.

## Ethical principles

### 1. Earth stewardship first

Protect and restore Earth's biosphere. No claim about interstellar survival excuses avoidable ecological harm on our home planet. Conservation, resilience, and biological understanding are valuable outcomes in their own right.

### 2. Dual benefit

Favor research that produces credible knowledge, technologies, education, or conservation benefits now, independently of speculative long-term deployment. Specify measurable present-day value rather than merely asserting it.

### 3. Scientific integrity and humility

Distinguish established findings from hypotheses and aspirations. Welcome criticism, disclose uncertainty, and do not exaggerate threats or promise that dispersal of life will succeed.

### 4. Planetary protection and non-contamination

Space exploration can create risks of **forward contamination** (Earth organisms or biological material reaching other environments) and **backward contamination** (potential extraterrestrial material returning to Earth). Research and mission concepts must consider applicable international obligations, planetary-protection policies, containment, biosafety, and scientific integrity.

The absence of detected extraterrestrial life is not proof that an environment is lifeless.

### 5. Respect for independent life and environments

A desire to preserve Earth life does not automatically authorize introducing it elsewhere. Indigenous organisms, prebiotic environments, and scientifically valuable pristine settings may have interests or values we do not yet understand. Assess ecological consequences and irreversible losses.

### 6. Governance across borders and generations

No individual or informal research network should claim unilateral authority to undertake irreversible biological interventions beyond Earth. Such decisions require transparent evidence, broad international deliberation, legitimate governance, and accountability to people and generations affected.

### 7. Restraint regarding Flowering

Rocks2Space may study the possibility of intentional life dispersal, but **research is not permission to deploy**. Any proposed future intervention must pass independent scientific, ecological, legal, and ethical review. Some destinations or interventions may never be justifiable.

## A practical decision framework

For each project or proposed intervention, ask:

1. **Present impact:** What benefits or harms to existing life, ecosystems, and people are plausible now?
2. **Evidence:** Which claims are demonstrated, modeled, uncertain, or speculative?
3. **Future consequences:** What long-term opportunities and irreversible risks might follow?
4. **Affected environments:** Could the work contaminate or alter another world, including one with undetected life?
5. **Alternatives:** Can the same knowledge or benefit be achieved through safer, reversible, or non-deployment approaches?
6. **Authority:** What oversight, legal obligations, international consultation, and consent processes are appropriate?
7. **Review:** What observations or new evidence would change the decision?

This framework does not turn moral uncertainty into a mechanical score. It makes assumptions, trade-offs, and reasons visible for challenge and revision.

## Research questions

- How should duties to existing organisms be weighed against benefits for distant future life?
- What precaution is justified when extraterrestrial ecosystems or prebiotic environments are poorly understood?
- Can long-term preservation projects be designed to reinforce biodiversity conservation today?
- What standards should govern containment, experimental work, mission design, and potential release?
- Who could legitimately authorize irreversible actions with intergenerational or planetary consequences?
- When should uncertainty lead to further study, postponement, or rejection?
