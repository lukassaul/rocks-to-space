# Soil as a Living Planetary System: The State of Soil Science and the Case for Preserving Earth's Soils

**Rocks2Space — Impact / Societal Benefits / Earth-First Stewardship**  
**Literature-based research review | November 2026 | Working draft**

> **Premise:** We cannot seriously discuss preserving or spreading complex life without understanding the living systems that make Earth habitable. Soil is one of those systems. Preserving Earth's soils has immediate value regardless of whether interstellar biological transport ever becomes possible.

## Executive summary

Soil is not simply weathered rock, plant substrate, or a carbon reservoir. It is a **structured, porous, chemically reactive and biologically inhabited interface** among lithosphere, atmosphere, hydrosphere and biosphere. It stores and moves water; transforms nutrients; supports vegetation and food production; filters and sometimes transmits contaminants; stores and releases carbon; and provides habitat for extraordinary, incompletely catalogued biodiversity.

Modern soil science combines pedology, mineralogy, physics, hydrology, geochemistry, microbiology, ecology, agronomy, remote sensing and social science. The science is mature in some domains—soil classification, many nutrient transformations, erosion mechanisms—and incomplete in others, particularly microbial functional diversity, causal soil-health indicators, deep-soil processes, long-term carbon persistence, and the ecological consequences of climate and land-use change.

The **FAO Status of the World's Soil Resources 2026** concludes that sustainable soil management has not expanded at the scale and pace needed to reverse widespread degradation. The assessment emphasizes the gap between available knowledge and implementation, while recognizing substantial regional differences [1]. A 2024 synthesis reported an estimate of **33% of soils moderately to highly degraded** [2]; this is a source-specific global estimate, not a universally measured fraction, and it should not be conflated with the 2026 assessment's management-adoption metrics.

**Rocks2Space's Earth-first conclusion:** understanding, monitoring and restoring soil is independently important, and knowledge of functional ecosystems is relevant to any future closed biosphere or Noah's Ark architecture. The latter connection is a research motivation, not evidence that soil can be recreated easily elsewhere.

## 1. What is soil?

Soils form through interactions among parent material, climate, organisms, topography, time, and human activity. They develop horizons and spatial structure through weathering, organic matter accumulation, biological mixing, leaching, precipitation, erosion and deposition. The classic state-factor framework associated with Hans Jenny remains a useful organizing model, though contemporary pedology also considers feedbacks, disturbance, historical contingencies and human-made soils.

A soil profile may include organic horizons, mineral surface horizons, zones of eluviation and illuviation, weathered parent material, and deeper substrates. Not all soils have all horizons. Wetlands, deserts, forests, grasslands, peatlands, volcanic soils, permafrost soils, croplands and urban soils have very different properties and functions.

### 1.1 The physical system

- **Texture:** relative sand, silt and clay content influences pore geometry, water retention and nutrient interactions.
- **Structure:** aggregates, biopores, cracks and root channels govern infiltration, aeration, erosion and root growth.
- **Bulk density and compaction:** affect pore space and plant access to water and oxygen.
- **Hydraulic properties:** infiltration, conductivity and retention vary with water potential, saturation and soil structure.
- **Temperature and freezing:** influence microbial rates, water movement and mechanical stability.

Soil water is not a single accessible pool: matric forces, osmotic conditions and pore connectivity govern whether roots and organisms can use it.

### 1.2 The chemical system

Clay minerals, organic matter and oxide surfaces bind ions and organic compounds. pH, redox potential, cation-exchange capacity, salinity and mineral weathering control nutrient availability and contaminant mobility. The same soil can immobilize a contaminant under one redox condition and release it under another.

Nutrient supply must be considered as **stocks, chemical forms and fluxes**, not merely fertilizer inputs. Nitrogen fixation, nitrification, denitrification, phosphorus sorption and mineralization are examples of transformations that couple chemistry to microbial ecology.

### 1.3 The biological system

Soils contain bacteria, archaea, fungi, protists, nematodes, mites, springtails, insects, earthworms, roots and many other organisms. These communities decompose organic matter, transform nutrients, form symbioses, create and modify pores, influence plant health, and participate in disease suppression or transmission. Soil organisms also compete, prey on one another and respond to moisture, temperature, oxygen and disturbance.

Soil biodiversity is **functional as well as taxonomic**: the presence of a species does not prove that a process is occurring at an ecologically meaningful rate. Functional redundancy may buffer some processes, but it is not unlimited and varies by environment [3, 4].

## 2. What soils do for Earth

| Function | Main mechanisms | Why preservation matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plant growth and food production | Water, anchorage, nutrients, microbial symbiosis | Food systems and terrestrial ecosystems |
| Water regulation | Infiltration, storage, drainage, preferential flow | Flooding, drought, groundwater recharge, water quality |
| Nutrient cycling | Weathering, decomposition, mineralization, redox transformations | Ecosystem productivity and pollution control |
| Carbon storage and exchange | Plant inputs, microbial processing, mineral association, decomposition | Climate feedbacks and soil fertility |
| Biodiversity habitat | Physical pore networks, gradients and resources | Food webs, resilience, evolutionary diversity |
| Contaminant transformation | Sorption, precipitation, degradation, transport | Human health and environmental protection |
| Physical landscape stability | Aggregation, root reinforcement, erosion resistance | Watersheds, agriculture, infrastructure |
| Cultural and historical record | Soil horizons, buried materials and geochemical archives | Archaeology, landscape history and knowledge |

These functions can conflict. Maximizing crop yield in the short term may degrade biodiversity or water quality; maximizing carbon storage is not the same as maximizing habitat diversity. **No single soil-health score captures every legitimate function.**

## 3. The state of the science in 2026

### 3.1 Global assessment and monitoring

The FAO's 2026 global assessment updates the 2015 *Status of the World's Soil Resources*, draws on literature from 2015–2025 and regional expertise, and emphasizes the insufficient adoption of sustainable soil management [1]. Its regional structure is important: soil threats and effective responses vary strongly with climate, geology, land use, institutions and livelihoods.

A 2024 peer-reviewed review synthesized threats including erosion, organic matter loss, nutrient imbalance, salinization, acidification, contamination, biodiversity loss, sealing, compaction and water stress [2]. Global estimates are necessarily uncertain because monitoring networks, definitions, sampling depth and data coverage differ.

**Interpretation rule:** do not treat *soil degradation*, *land degradation*, *declining agricultural productivity* and *low adoption of management practices* as interchangeable statistics.

### 3.2 Soil biodiversity: remarkable progress, large blind spots

The FAO's 2020 *State of Knowledge of Soil Biodiversity* assembled a broad synthesis of belowground organisms, threats and ecosystem functions [3]. A 2026 review in *Nature Reviews Biodiversity* describes advances in connecting soil organisms to nutrient cycling, decomposition, plant productivity, climate regulation and pathogen control, while identifying unresolved climate feedbacks, urban soil ecology and soil-health indicators [4].

DNA metabarcoding, metagenomics, metatranscriptomics, microscopy, stable-isotope probing and controlled microcosms reveal different facets of the system. A sequencing read is not a direct count of living organisms or a measurement of process rate. Reference databases and extraction biases limit taxonomic inference; spatial and temporal variability complicate comparisons.

**Research gap:** globally coordinated, repeated measurements that combine biodiversity, function, soil properties and land-management history.

### 3.3 Soil carbon: stocks are measurable; permanence is harder

Soil organic carbon is a mixture of plant- and microbe-derived compounds in diverse physical and chemical environments. Carbon persistence depends on accessibility to decomposers, aggregation, mineral associations, moisture, temperature and environmental conditions—not simply a universally stable molecular category. Mineral-associated organic matter and particulate organic matter are useful operational fractions, but their dynamics differ across soils.

An elementary stock estimate is

\[
SOC_{\rm stock}=\sum_j \rho_{b,j}\,d_j\,c_j\,(1-f_{{\rm coarse},j}),
\]

where \(\rho_b\) is dry bulk density, \(d\) layer thickness, \(c\) organic carbon mass fraction and \(f_{\rm coarse}\) coarse-fragment volume fraction, with consistent units and measurement conventions. Comparing sites or years requires attention to equivalent soil mass, depth, bulk-density change and spatial variability.

The IPCC finds that several land-management practices can reduce erosion and nutrient loss and may build soil carbon, but outcomes depend on local conditions and have limits [5]. Soil carbon sequestration should not be presented as unlimited, permanent or interchangeable with rapid fossil-carbon emissions reductions. Evaluate greenhouse gases **including N₂O and CH₄**, leakage, saturation, reversibility and measurement uncertainty.

### 3.4 Erosion, compaction and hydrology

Water and wind erosion remove soil material and redistribute carbon and nutrients. Rates depend on rainfall and wind, vegetation cover, slope, texture, aggregation, land management and extreme events. Tillage erosion, landslides and harvesting-related soil loss can also matter. Soil formation may be slow relative to severe erosion, but rates vary substantially.

Compaction and surface sealing can reduce infiltration, root penetration and gas exchange, increasing runoff and altering habitat. Restoration should track physical function, not only chemical fertility.

### 3.5 Salinity, acidification and contamination

Salinization and sodicity can emerge from irrigation, drainage limitations, sea-water intrusion, geology and climatic drying. Acidification can arise from natural processes and land management. Metals, excess nutrients, pesticides, hydrocarbons, PFAS and other contaminants require substance-specific assessments; persistence, toxicity and mobility depend on soil chemistry and exposure routes.

Bioremediation and phytoremediation may help in selected settings but can transfer contaminants between compartments rather than destroy them. Soil restoration must include contaminant fate and human exposure, not merely plant regrowth.

### 3.6 Climate and soil feedbacks

Climate change alters precipitation extremes, soil temperature, freeze–thaw, fire regimes, vegetation, microbial activity and erosion. Thawing permafrost and drained peatlands are particularly important carbon-related concerns. Feedback magnitude and timing remain uncertain and vary by landscape.

The IPCC's *Climate Change and Land* assessment evaluates land degradation, sustainable management, food security and greenhouse gas interactions [5]. The IPBES land-degradation assessment situates soils within broader biodiversity, ecosystem services and human well-being [6].

## 4. Major threats and evidence-based responses

| Threat | Potential responses | Important caveat |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Water/wind erosion | Ground cover, contour practices, shelterbelts, suitable rotations, landscape restoration | Results depend on slope, storm intensity, land use and local ecology |
| Organic matter depletion | Organic inputs, cover crops, diversified rotations, reduced disturbance where suitable | Carbon gains are context-specific and may plateau or reverse |
| Nutrient imbalance | Soil testing, integrated nutrient management, precision application, recycling | Both nutrient deficits and excesses cause harm |
| Compaction | Traffic management, reduced loads when wet, root systems, targeted remediation | Deep compaction can persist; intervention may disturb soil |
| Salinity/sodicity | Water management, drainage, salt balance, appropriate amendments | Drainage can transfer salts downstream and consume scarce water |
| Acidification | Diagnose causes, appropriate liming where indicated | Excess liming can impair other functions |
| Contamination | Source control, risk-based remediation, containment and monitoring | Excavation can destroy soil structure and habitat |
| Biodiversity loss | Habitat diversity, reduced unnecessary disturbance, pollution reduction | Biological responses vary; no universal inoculant fixes every soil |
| Soil sealing | Avoid unnecessary paving, protect high-function soils, carefully restore disturbed sites | De-sealing alone does not instantly recreate mature soil |
| Peatland drainage | Rewetting and appropriate land-use transitions | Methane dynamics and local livelihoods require consideration |

The strongest general conclusion is **prevent avoidable damage before relying on expensive reconstruction**. A century-old soil profile or peatland ecosystem is not recreated simply by adding compost.

The FAO 2026 assessment emphasizes that many useful management practices already exist, but institutional, financial and regional constraints limit uptake [1]. Implementation requires farmer and landholder participation, extension services, secure tenure, policy, finance and monitoring—not just laboratory discovery.

## 5. How we know: methods, measurements and limitations

A serious soil-conservation research program needs multiple scales:

**Field description and sampling:** horizons, texture, structure, color, roots, bulk density, pH, electrical conductivity, nutrients, carbon and contaminants. Sample depth and season must be recorded.

**Physical measurements:** infiltration, saturated and unsaturated conductivity, water retention, aggregate stability, compaction and erosion.

**Biological measurements:** biomass, respiration, enzyme activities, fauna, roots, sequencing, functional assays and pathogen surveillance. Each proxy has limitations; no single biological metric defines health.

**Landscape observation:** remote sensing, land-cover change, terrain models, erosion mapping, soil spectroscopy, geophysics and long-term experimental sites. Satellites infer many soil properties indirectly; calibration and ground truth are essential.

**Causal evaluation:** randomized or matched management comparisons where feasible, replicated field trials, before/after monitoring, and explicit confounders. A greener field is not automatically a healthier soil.

**Data practice:** georeferenced metadata, methods, uncertainty, sampling depth, land-use history, data licenses and repeatability. Respect Indigenous and local knowledge, landowner consent, and sensitive-site data.

### Minimum monitoring dashboard

A practical program could report: soil organic carbon stock by depth; bulk density; infiltration; aggregate stability; pH and salinity; nutrient balance; erosion or ground cover; selected biological function metrics; biodiversity indicators; and locally relevant contamination risks. Select indicators according to the soil's intended functions and regional baseline.

## 6. Research frontiers and open questions

1. **Biodiversity–function causality:** which organisms and interactions are essential, redundant or context-dependent?
2. **Microbial dark matter:** how much of soil biodiversity and metabolic capacity remains uncultured or poorly classified?
3. **Carbon persistence:** how do mineral surfaces, microbial products, aggregation and disturbance determine long-term stocks?
4. **Deep soils:** how much biology, carbon and weathering activity are missed by shallow sampling?
5. **Climate extremes:** how do drought–rewetting, flooding, heat and fire alter soil resilience?
6. **Soil recovery:** what functions return quickly after restoration, and which require decades or longer?
7. **Urban and contaminated soils:** how can cities preserve soil functions while managing exposure risks?
8. **Monitoring:** can cheap, repeatable indicators reliably detect meaningful changes rather than sampling noise?
9. **Regional equity:** how can conservation practices work for different land-tenure, livelihood and cultural settings?
10. **Ecological assembly:** can complex soil communities be reconstructed after disturbance, and which historical properties cannot be recovered?

## 7. Why soil science belongs in Rocks2Space

### 7.1 Earth first: preservation is a mission outcome

Rocks2Space's long-term interest in life's continuity gives a direct reason to study the systems that already sustain life. Soil degradation threatens food, water, biodiversity and ecological resilience **now**. Research and outreach that improve soil understanding need no interstellar justification.

### 7.2 From living rock to living soil

The [Living Rock](../../../Payload/Living_Rock_Radiolytic_Ecosystems/README.md) explores whether mineral–water–microbe systems could sustain limited metabolism over very long durations. Soil science demonstrates how intricate these relationships become on Earth: mineral surfaces, pore water, redox gradients, microbial interactions and nutrient flows jointly shape biological activity. It does **not** prove that an isolated interstellar rock could support a functioning soil-like ecology.

### 7.3 From Noah's Ark to ecological infrastructure

A [full biological ark](../../../Payload/Noahs_Ark_and_Artificial_Worlds/README.md) cannot be designed as a species catalogue alone. Plants may require soil structure, nutrient cycles, microbial partners, decomposers and physical water regulation. A functioning soil ecosystem is not just a bag of sterilized mineral grains plus a microbial inoculum.

Soil studies can inform **closed-habitat experiments**, but terrestrial soils are open systems connected to climate, bedrock, water and regional biota. Their behavior cannot be transferred uncritically to a spacecraft.

### 7.4 Planetary science and planetary protection

Regolith on another world is not automatically soil in the ecological sense. Some scientific classification systems use broader definitions, so the terminology should be specified. Studying how Earth's soils emerge from rock and biology may clarify what a living planetary surface requires—but **does not justify introducing organisms into extraterrestrial environments**.

## 8. A practical Earth-first outreach and research agenda

**Project A — Soil literacy.** Publish an accessible guide to horizons, texture, pores, microbes, nutrient cycles, carbon, water and erosion, with annotated local field examples and vetted public resources.

**Project B — Open soil-data atlas.** Curate links to national and international soil maps and monitoring programs, document coverage and uncertainty, and avoid claiming satellite imagery directly measures soil health.

**Project C — Long-term soil observation.** Collaborate with soil scientists, farms, land trusts, schools and conservation groups on standardized, non-invasive or minimally invasive monitoring. Record baseline conditions before interventions.

**Project D — Biodiversity and function.** Review methods linking soil organisms to decomposition, nutrient cycling and plant performance. Prefer contained laboratory studies and existing data; avoid environmental releases of nonnative organisms.

**Project E — Soil carbon accounting.** Develop reproducible examples using depth, bulk density, equivalent soil mass, uncertainty and multiple greenhouse gases.

**Project F — Restoration evidence review.** Compare interventions by soil type, climate, land use, cost, ecological outcome and time horizon; report failures and trade-offs.

**Project G — Artificial biosphere translation.** Build a conceptual nutrient/water/microbe dependency model for a small closed habitat, while keeping Earth-conservation objectives independent.

### Suggested deliverables

- A public-facing primer: *Soil Is a Living System*.
- A curated, regularly updated bibliography and data-resource guide.
- An indicator dictionary distinguishing measured quantities from proxies.
- A reproducible soil-carbon stock calculation notebook.
- A review of soil biodiversity monitoring limitations.
- A comparison of restoration outcomes by ecosystem and region.

## 10. Editorial and ethical boundaries

- Soil health is **contextual**; avoid declaring all natural soils unhealthy because they are unproductive for crops.
- Preserve natural biodiversity and local ecological knowledge; do not equate restoration with importing a standardized microbiome.
- Separate measured changes from modeled estimates, and soil-specific findings from global extrapolations.
- A useful soil-science collaboration must provide independent benefits to Earth, even if Rocks2Space's long-term transport concepts remain speculative.
- No section of this paper advocates releasing engineered or nonnative organisms into natural soils or extraterrestrial environments.

**Research status:** literature-grounded overview and outreach agenda. Next steps are a systematic bibliography, geographically differentiated case studies, and expert review by soil scientists.
